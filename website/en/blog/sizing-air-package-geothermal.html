Sizing an Air Package for Geothermal Drilling

A wrongly sized air package burns money in two ways: an undersized spread can't clean the hole and stalls progress; an oversized one pays rent and fuel for nothing. Here's how we size a geothermal air package, step by step.

Note: This is a simplified pre-sizing method. On a real project, water inflow, temperature profile and foam use change the numbers materially. Send us your well program — we run the full calculation free of charge.

Step 1 — The target: annular velocity

Cuttings ride to surface on air velocity in the annulus. The industry floor for dry air is roughly 3,000 ft/min (15 m/s); raise the target when water inflow or coarse cuttings are expected. Required flow scales with the annular cross-section:

Q ≈ V × A × (pressure/temperature corrections)
A = π/4 × (Dhole² − Dpipe²)

Hole size dominates the flow demand: a 17½" section needs roughly four times the air of an 8½" section. That's why wide top-hole sections run multi-compressor spreads that shed units as the hole gets smaller.

Step 2 — Flow by hole section

Rough values for a typical geothermal program (5" drill pipe, dry air, sea level):

Once water inflow starts, these numbers stop being enough — every cubic meter per hour of water steals transport capacity. The practical fix is usually foam injection, not more flow.

Step 3 — Pressure components

  • Hydrostatic head in the hole: every 10 m of water column ≈ 1 bar (every 33 ft ≈ 14.5 psi). In deep geothermal this is the dominant term.
  • Annular friction: grows with depth and flow.
  • DTH hammer operating pressure: typically 250–500 psi when a hammer is on bottom.
  • Surface line losses: manifold, hoses, standpipe — small but not zero.

A well that must unload 300 m of static water column faces ~430 psi from hydrostatics alone; with friction and the hammer, totals easily land in the 700–1,000 psi band. This is exactly why the 350–500 psi ceiling of primary compressors runs out — and where the booster comes in.

Step 4 — Altitude and temperature derating

Catalog ratings are at sea level. Much of Turkey's geothermal country sits at 200–1,000 m elevation; Central Anatolian sites exceed 1,200 m. Rule of thumb: derate capacity ~3–4% per 300 m (1,000 ft) of elevation, and remember the diesel engine derates too. Skipping a 10–12% margin at a 1,000 m site comes back as missing flow at the wellhead.

Step 5 — Unit combination (worked example)

Note the booster ceiling sits well above the calculated need. That's not luxury — it's insurance: an unexpected inflow or a deeper target won't force a package swap mid-well.

Three field warnings

  1. Don't upsize the booster without upsizing the feed. System flow equals the sum of the primaries; a big booster on a small feed does small work.
  2. Plan redundancy. Two mid-size compressors usually beat one large one: when a unit fails, the operation limps on instead of stopping.
  3. Optimize well cost, not day rate. A cheaper package that slows penetration by 20% is the expensive package.

The sizing order never changes: flow by section, then pressure components, then altitude derating, then the unit combination. Have a well program ready? Send it over — we'll return the math and a combination proposal.
